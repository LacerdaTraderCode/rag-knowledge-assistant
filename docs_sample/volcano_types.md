# Types of Volcanoes

Volcanoes are usually grouped by the shape they build, and that shape reflects the kind of magma behind it. Shield volcanoes, such as Mauna Loa in Hawaii, are broad and gently sloping because they are built from thin, runny basaltic lava that flows a long way before it cools. Their eruptions tend to be effusive, with lava pouring out rather than exploding.

Stratovolcanoes, also called composite volcanoes, are steep, symmetrical cones made of alternating layers of lava and ash. Their magma is thick and sticky and traps gas, so pressure builds until the eruption becomes explosive. Mount Fuji and Mount St. Helens, which erupted violently in 1980, are stratovolcanoes.

Cinder cones are the smallest type: steep piles of loose fragments thrown out around a single vent. They often erupt only once and stop within a few years. A caldera forms when a very large eruption empties the magma chamber and the ground above collapses into a wide basin.
