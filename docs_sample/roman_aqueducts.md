# Roman Aqueducts

Roman engineers carried water to cities over long distances using nothing but gravity. The channel was laid with a gentle, steady downward slope, in some cases only a few tenths of a percent, so the water kept flowing without ever rushing. Most of the route ran underground or along the surface, where it was cheaper and easier to maintain.

Where the land dipped, arches lifted the channel across valleys. The Pont du Gard in southern France, about 49 meters tall and built in the first century, is among the best preserved examples. Settling tanks along the way let sediment drop out so the water arriving in the city was clearer.

The first aqueduct serving Rome, the Aqua Appia, was completed in 312 BC, and by the third century AD the city was supplied by eleven of them. The water fed public fountains, baths, and some wealthy households, and the surplus flushed the sewers.
