# Compound Interest

Compound interest is interest earned on both the original amount and on the interest already added. A deposit of 1,000 at 7 percent a year grows to about 1,967 after ten years, whereas simple interest, which only ever pays on the original amount, would give 1,700. The gap widens every year because the growing balance is what earns the next payment.

The rule of 72 gives a quick estimate of how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual rate in percent. At 6 percent the answer is about twelve years, and at 9 percent about eight. The rule is an approximation that works best for rates between roughly 6 and 10 percent.

How often interest is compounded, monthly rather than yearly, matters far less than the rate and the time. Time is the strongest lever, so starting early beats contributing more later. The effect works against borrowers too: a credit card balance that compounds at a high rate can grow faster than the minimum payments shrink it.
